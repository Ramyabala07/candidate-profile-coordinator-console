# Show picks above the list, comment on review

For a 548px panel where a coordinator adds 1–5 accommodations from a 30–200+ item catalogue, the best-supported design has two states, as Google Drive's share dialog does. The first is a **browse state**: search, the category tab strip, and a grouped checkbox list where picked items stay ticked in place, plus a **compact, capped selection strip above the list**. The second is a **compose state** reached with Review. It lists each pick with Remove, holds the comment, and ends in a button that names the action ("Add 3 accommodations"). The evidence is consistent on one point. Users must see the actual selected items, not just a count, and they must see them outside the scrolling list. Baymard found that a bare count "performed poorly in testing", and NN/g recommends tokens above the list. The dual listbox, the textbook answer for "review before committing", does not fit 548px. Stacked vertically, it turns into "selected items above the list" anyway. The comment is better placed in the compose state than squeezed under the picker. A block editor is the most expensive element in the vertical budget, and Drive, Luma and Workable all use a separate compose or review state when a message goes with the selection. The main risk is that NN/g warns against confirmation steps for routine actions. The compose state avoids that trap only if it does real work (the comment, removal, warnings) and does not just ask "Are you sure?". No public product combines a 200-item grouped catalogue, a visible selection and a comment in one narrow panel, so this recommendation is assembled from adjacent precedents, not copied from one product.

## The evidence agrees: users need the actual items, shown outside the scroll

The strongest finding comes from Baymard's large-scale e-commerce testing of applied filters, which is structurally the same problem: many options, a few picked, and a need to confirm and remove. Without an overview of the applied selections, users struggled to tell what was applied and to remove items. Those who missed one applied filter misjudged the whole result set. **Showing only a count, such as "Filter (3)", performed poorly** because users still had to open the interface to see or change anything ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/how-to-design-applied-filters)). NN/g reaches the same conclusion for listboxes. In a scrolling list, users miss selected options that have scrolled out of view, so designers should "show selected items above the listbox as tokens or highlight the selected items in a nonscrollable list" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/listbox-dropdown/)). This also follows from NN/g's visibility-of-system-status heuristic: a selection should never silently drop out of view ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visibility-system-status/)).

Two more findings shape the details. First, **the picked item should also stay ticked where it is in the list**. Baymard found that 97% of benchmarked sites keep a selected option in its original position, and Maersk's design system says to highlight selections "both in their original placement, as well as in the applied filters section" ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/how-to-design-applied-filters); [Maersk](https://designsystem.maersk.com/guidelines/search-filter-and-sort/filter-patterns/)). The two signals work together: one confirms the click, the other summarises the set. Second, **where the overview sits matters less than whether it exists**. Baymard tested three placements and found the results "inconclusive" ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/how-to-design-applied-filters)). That leaves room to place the strip wherever the panel's vertical budget allows.

Search makes this more pressing. GDS added a text filter to GOV.UK checkbox lists with hundreds of options and shipped a rule that **"checked checkboxes always remain visible, regardless of the filter"**. A hidden live region announced both the match count and the selected count ([GOV.UK Accessibility blog](https://accessibility.blog.gov.uk/2019/04/08/accessibility-lessons-dealing-with-a-large-amount-of-form-inputs/)). The same team rejected an autocomplete multi-select that showed selections below the text box, because users could not see what they had picked. In a picker with tabs and search, picks from another tab or hidden by the current search are exactly the ones that go missing. A strip above the list protects them without mixing non-matching rows into the search results.

## Five patterns fit a 548px panel; the dual listbox does not

| Pattern | Precedent | Fits 548px? | Strength | Weakness for this case |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dual listbox / transfer list | Ant Transfer, Salesforce dueling picklist | No, not side by side | Best side-by-side review before committing | Two 250px columns leave about 12–14 characters per line and 5–6 visible rows. Salesforce tags its version "Desktop Only". |
| Count tag in the field | Carbon MultiSelect | Yes | Fixed size, no layout shift | Hides which items are picked, which Baymard found performs poorly |
| Chips inside the field, wrap and grow | Atlassian Select, Fluent TagPicker, Drive, Slack | Yes | Familiar from share dialogs | Grows without limit and assumes typeahead, not browsing. Tied to a combobox (a dropdown). |
| Capped selection strip between search/tabs and list | Salesforce Add Items, Baymard, NN/g | Yes | Item names always visible, one-click removal | Each new row pushes the list down unless height is reserved |
| Selected items pinned to the top of the list | Primer `showSelectedOptionsFirst`, Carbon on reopen | Yes | No extra chrome | Items jump away from the pointer if reordered live. Lost under search filtering. |
| "Selected (n)" tab beside the category tabs | Shopify "Included / Excluded / All" | Yes | Full review without leaving the picker, no chips | Hidden behind a click, so it is effectively a count until opened |
| Two states: browse, then review/compose | Google Drive compose state, Luma, Workable | Yes | Room for comment, removal and warnings | Adds a step. Risks reading as a routine confirmation. |

The dual listbox is the one pattern the brief seems to call for and the one that fails on width. Ant's tokens put each column at 180px by default or 250px large, with a 200px default height. The docs describe Transfer as taking "up more space" ([Ant Design Transfer](https://ant.design/components/transfer)). Salesforce's dueling picklist is tagged "Desktop Only" ([SLDS v1](https://v1.lightningdesignsystem.com/components/dueling-picklist/)), and KDE justifies the pattern by the need to see selected and available items at once ([KDE HIG](https://develop.kde.org/hig/patterns-content/duallist)). Its useful idea, a separate always-visible target list with a count header, carries over only when stacked. Stacked, it becomes the selection strip. Vitaly Friedman also positions the dual listbox for bulk roles-and-permissions work ([Smart Interface Design Patterns](https://smart-interface-design-patterns.com/articles/combobox-multiselect-listbox/)), which is far heavier than 1–5 picks.

In-field chips (Atlassian, Fluent) and Carbon's count tag both depend on a combobox, which is a dropdown. That conflicts with the console's no-dropdown rule, and Atlassian's own guidance says to "use checkboxes for options that require a button press to apply the setting" ([Atlassian Select](https://atlassian.design/components/select/usage)). Fluent's tags "wrap and will make the height grow to fit the content" with no truncation built in ([Fluent 2 TagPicker](https://fluent2.microsoft.design/components/web/react/core/tagpicker/usage)). That growth is a problem in a panel whose list is already competing for height.

Pinning selected items to the top is a real, documented option ([Primer SelectPanel](https://primer.style/product/components/select-panel/)). Carbon's version is the safe one: selected items move to the top only **when the menu reopens**, not as the user ticks ([Carbon Dropdown](https://carbondesignsystem.com/components/dropdown/usage/)). Live reordering would move the row away from the pointer just after the click, which conflicts with the stay-in-place behaviour Baymard found nearly universal. No study tests pinning directly. Pinning also does nothing for picks hidden by a search or sitting on another tab.

The "Selected (n)" tab is the cleanest no-chip option. Shopify uses Included / Excluded / All segmented tabs to turn a selection into a filter ([Mobbin: Shopify new catalog](https://mobbin.com/screens/0a7b552d-5351-449e-aa91-f5ef1b2a6d97)). It slots into an existing horizontal tab strip with no new chrome. While the coordinator browses another tab, though, the selection shows as a number, which is the case Baymard found weak. It works as a backup review view, not as the main way to keep picks visible.

## A capped strip above the list costs one row and stops picks going missing

The closest enterprise precedent is Salesforce's "Add Items" picker. It has a category rail with count badges, a "Search all items…" field, and **directly under the search** a row of selected chips with "1 item selected". The long list follows, and the primary button repeats the count: "Add 1 Nav Item" ([Mobbin: Salesforce Add Items](https://mobbin.com/screens/1b9eaaed-13d9-4e6d-ba85-ed05059250a9)). That dialog is about 800px wide with its rail; the width is inferred from a screenshot. Drop the rail and turn the categories into the existing tab strip, and it fits 548px. Google AI Studio's roughly 520px model-selection panel shows that search plus category filter chips fit at this width, and Quicken's roughly 425px side panel shows a grouped checkbox tree does too ([Mobbin: Google AI Studio](https://mobbin.com/screens/26756ff2-8eb4-4476-a9cd-055bc11c1fbf); [Mobbin: Quicken](https://mobbin.com/screens/ee9bca99-93ef-45d4-888f-30d1c51efc10)). Both widths are inferred and accurate to about ±10%.

The cost is vertical space and layout shift. Baymard notes that each new row of selections pushes the content below it down. On desktop its fix is to truncate rows 2 and beyond. In narrow mobile containers it either caps the area at 1–2 rows with a "See all" control, or uses one horizontally scrolling row with three overflow cues: a half-cut last chip, a faded edge and a count ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/how-to-design-applied-filters)). Carbon sets the outer limit at "Do not wrap tags in a group of more than five lines", keeps tag titles "under 20 characters", and uses 8px gaps ([Carbon Tag](https://carbondesignsystem.com/components/tag/usage/)). At 548px, about three or four chips of that length fit per row (this is arithmetic, not a sourced figure). "Extra Time +25%", "Head Covering", "Inhaler" and "Screen Reader" all fit in one row, and a typical 1–5 pick set never needs more than two.

Web.dev's layout-shift guidance says shifts right after user input are "generally fine" and excludes them from CLS ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/cls)). But CLS measures page stability, not click accuracy. If the strip grows when a box is ticked, the next row moves just as the coordinator reaches for it. That misclick risk is an inference, and no study measures it. Reserving the strip's height avoids it. Give it a fixed one-row slot that shows a quiet empty state when nothing is picked, let it grow to a second row, and past that show "+N more", which opens the review state. Spectrum's `maxRows` with an expand button documents the same mechanism ([React Spectrum TagGroup](https://react-spectrum.adobe.com/react-spectrum/TagGroup.html)). The space budget holds up. At a 900px panel height, header, search, tabs, a one-row strip and the footer leave roughly 12–14 list rows. At about 700px the list drops to 7–9 rows, which is the point to stop adding chrome. These figures are the research team's own arithmetic, not a published threshold. NN/g's warning that long, narrow scroll regions are harder to steer through ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/listbox-dropdown/)) argues for keeping the list as tall as possible, and that is the main reason the comment should not live in this view.

Three supporting behaviours close the gaps. Changing the search or the tab never clears picks. The strip, not the filtered list, is where out-of-view picks live, so the search results stay clean (the GDS keep-checked-rows-visible approach works too, but needs a "Selected" divider so those rows do not look like matches). A per-tab count badge ("Assistive Technology · 1") signals picks on other tabs. No design system documents this, so it is a local extension, with Salesforce's category count badges as the nearest precedent. A small team caution applies. If the console's "no chips" rule, set because a chip grid could not hold 20–25 options, also covers the summary, a one-line text list of names with a "Change" link meets the same evidence bar. What matters is that the names are visible, not that they are drawn as chips.

## The comment belongs in a compose state, not under the picker

Share and invite dialogs are the closest precedent for "a selection plus a message", and they divide in an instructive way. Single-view dialogs stack chips, then the message, then the action: Salesforce Files puts a chip field above "Add a message…", and Asana hides the message behind "Customize message" ([Mobbin: Salesforce share](https://mobbin.com/screens/e4175449-83cd-4f2b-9729-8bf8a354eaf1); [Mobbin: Asana](https://mobbin.com/screens/55ab23c3-d773-4a97-8400-1380330ba923)). Those dialogs pick recipients by typeahead and have no browsable list competing for height. Google Drive, at roughly 490px and almost exactly this panel's width, uses **two states in one fixed surface**. After the first pick the access list disappears, a back arrow appears, and the dialog shows chips, a "Notify people" checkbox and a Message textarea above Cancel / Send ([Mobbin: Drive compose state](https://mobbin.com/screens/828a1d54-ead3-487d-a8f7-e21dfcedc530)). Luma's invite flow gives the invitee list and the message their own step with Back ([Mobbin: Luma](https://mobbin.com/screens/20381215-5f38-4e32-a7dc-199d4d852f6f)). Workable's ATS invite adds an explicit prompt, "See if everything looks right, then send the invitations" ([Mobbin: Workable](https://mobbin.com/screens/e87543a9-c7b0-4d8d-8230-237f9b885882)). The pattern is consistent: when a message travels with the selection and a list has to be browsed, products give the message its own state.

The case against a second step is real and should shape the design. Nielsen argues that confirmations for routine actions train people to click through, and recommends restating the specific request and labelling the button with the outcome, never "Are you sure?" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/confirmation-dialog/)). Primer also warns against confirmations for low-risk, easily undone actions ([Primer ConfirmationDialog](https://primer.style/product/components/confirmation-dialog/guidelines)). GOV.UK's "Check answers" pattern supports a single review before committing a small-to-medium transaction, with a change control per item ([GOV.UK](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/check-answers/)). The two positions agree once the review state has a job to do. Here it does: it holds the comment, offers removal on each pick, and carries warnings such as the already-sat note. It is also the only place an accommodation's consequence (a changed exam timer, a candidate-facing change) can be stated before it applies. Primer's modal SelectPanel follows the same commit model, applying on the primary action and restoring the previous selection on cancel ([Primer SelectPanel](https://primer.style/product/components/select-panel/)). So the review state should read as composing the change, not confirming it. List each accommodation with Remove, give the comment field a clear label, and make the primary button state the outcome ("Add 3 accommodations"), as Salesforce's "Add 1 Nav Item" does. An optional count on the Review button ("Review (3)") is a label, not a footer summary, so it stays within the constraint. Removing the last item should return to the picker, not leave an empty review.

Outside the ATS world, exam-vendor and clinical tools point the same way. Pearson VUE's accommodation request form pairs each requested item with a written rationale ([RARS guide, 2021](https://www-tchs.stjohns.k12.fl.us/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/RARS_Accommodation_Guide_Candidate.pdf)), which matches a single shared comment. Epic's order composer keeps a running list of pending changes before Pend or Sign ([Brigham routing guide](https://www.brighamandwomens.org/assets/BWH/research/pdfs/14-research-id-med-orders-routing-to-in-basket-for-signature.pdf)), which is the "visible pending set, then commit" model, though in a much wider layout.

## Accessibility details that make the two-state flow hold up

Inside a panel (not a popup), the list works best as native checkboxes grouped in one fieldset per category, each with its legend. This follows GOV.UK, with "checkboxes to the left of their labels" for magnifier users ([GOV.UK Checkboxes](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/checkboxes/)). It gives Space-to-toggle for free and avoids the listbox restriction on interactive content inside options ([W3C APG](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/listbox/)). The tab strip follows the APG Tabs pattern, and switching tabs never moves focus unexpectedly or clears picks. Each item in the strip and on the review cards gets a remove button labelled with its item ("Remove Extra Time +25%"), following Visa's "clear {label}" convention ([Visa](https://design.visa.com/components/chips/accessibility/)). Removing an item also unticks its row. Where focus goes after a removal is contested: eBay says the next interactive element, CoreUI says an adjacent chip, and neither tested it ([eBay Evo](https://opensource.ebay.com/evo-web/components/chip/accessibility); [CoreUI](https://coreui.io/react/docs/forms/chip-input/accessibility/)). A sensible order is the next chip, then the previous chip, then the search field. One polite live region should announce what GDS shipped, the match count and the selected count, for example "Extra Time +25% added. 3 selected" ([GOV.UK Accessibility blog](https://accessibility.blog.gov.uk/2019/04/08/accessibility-lessons-dealing-with-a-large-amount-of-form-inputs/)). None of the design systems checked specifies this announcement, so it is a deliberate addition, not a borrowed standard. For a virtualised 200+ list, the APG's `aria-setsize`/`aria-posinset` applies. Primer turns virtualisation off for grouped lists ([Primer SelectPanel](https://primer.style/product/components/select-panel/)), so a grouped catalogue at the upper end needs testing for performance.

## Conclusion

The question assumed that seeing the selection and adding a comment have to share one view, and that the footer is the natural home for the summary. The evidence separates them. Visibility is needed continuously, while the coordinator browses, searches and switches tabs, so it belongs in the body: a reserved, capped strip of names above the list, with items ticked in place. The comment is needed once, at the moment of committing, so it belongs in a compose state that also lists the picks with Remove and names the outcome on its button. That turns the review step from a click-through confirmation into the place where the change is written, and keeps the picker's list as tall as a 548px panel allows. The current "Cancel / Review" footer with a review step already matches the second half. The gap is the first half: without an in-body strip, picks made on another tab or hidden by a search are invisible until Review.

The uncertainty lies in the details, not the direction. No controlled study compares chips with checkbox-only lists, tests pin-to-top against stay-in-place, or measures misclicks from a growing strip. Every surface width cited from screenshots is an estimate. No public product combines all of the brief's constraints. The cheapest way to settle the open points is a quick test of the two-state prototype with coordinators: pick three accommodations across two tabs with one search in between. Watch whether they check the strip before pressing Review, and whether anyone treats the review step as a speed bump.
